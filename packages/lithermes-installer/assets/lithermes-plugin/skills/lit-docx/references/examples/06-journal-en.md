# Background

Most primary school playgrounds in the study city are open asphalt laid decades ago. On a clear summer afternoon that surface absorbs sunlight for hours, and by the lunch break it can be far hotter than the air above it.

The study covered fourteen primary schools in 2025 and 2026. Every school had open asphalt, and most also had at least one other surface. Each surface was treated as a separate measured patch, so that surfaces could be compared within the same school on the same afternoon.

Table 1. Measured surface patches by type (sample data)

| Surface and cover | Schools with this patch | Typical patch area (m²) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Asphalt, unshaded | 14 | 600-1,800 |
| Asphalt under tree canopy | 9 | 150-400 |
| Asphalt under shade sail | 5 | 80-200 |
| Rubber safety surface, unshaded | 8 | 60-250 |
| Grass, unshaded | 11 | 300-1,200 |

Source: school site surveys, spring 2025 (sample)

## Measurement approach

A hot day was a school day on which the air temperature at the nearest weather station reached 30 °C; there were 23 in 2025 and 31 in 2026. A fixed infrared sensor above each patch logged surface temperature every ten minutes, and the 14:00 value was used for comparison. Active play is time a child spends running, climbing or in a ball game, recorded by observers who scanned set zones every five minutes during the lunch break.

# Surface temperatures

Surface type and shade made a large difference, and the ranking was the same in both summers. Unshaded rubber was hottest, which matters because it lies under climbing frames where children sit and put their hands. Shaded asphalt was coolest, about 20 °C below the same material in the sun.

Table 2. Median surface temperature at 14:00 on hot days (sample data)

| Surface and cover | 2025 (°C) | 2026 (°C) | Difference from unshaded asphalt, 2026 (°C) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Rubber safety surface, unshaded | 58 | 60 | +6 |
| Asphalt, unshaded | 52 | 54 | 0 |
| Grass, unshaded | 36 | 39 | -15 |
| Asphalt under shade sail | 38 | 39 | -15 |
| Asphalt under tree canopy | 33 | 34 | -20 |

Source: patch infrared sensors, summer terms 2025 and 2026 (sample)

Grass rose the most in the dry second summer. Sails and trees cooled the surface by similar amounts at midday, but a sail's shade moves off its patch as the sun moves west. By 15:30 the surface under sails had risen by a median of 6 °C, while under canopy it stayed within 2 °C of the 14:00 value.

# Use of the playground

Shade changed how children used the playground. Six schools had at least 30 % shade over their main play area, and eight had less than 10 %. On mild days active play was almost the same in both groups. On hot days it fell by nearly half in the low-shade schools.

Table 3. Active play per child during the lunch break (sample data)

| School group | Schools | Mild days (minutes) | Hot days (minutes) | Change on hot days (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Shade cover of 30 % or more | 6 | 24 | 21 | -13 |
| Shade cover below 10 % | 8 | 23 | 13 | -43 |

Note: mild days are school days with a maximum air temperature between 20 and 25 °C. Source: observer scans, summer terms 2025 and 2026 (sample)

In the low-shade schools, children crowded into narrow strips of shade along building walls, and play there was mostly sitting and talking. Five of those eight schools kept younger pupils indoors at lunch when the forecast passed 30 °C; none of the high-shade schools had such a rule.

# Cost and maintenance

The three measures a school could add within one summer holiday differ in cost, in how soon they work and in upkeep.

Table 4. Cost of cooling measures per 100 m² of playground (sample data)

| Measure | Installed cost (thousand) | Full effect | Annual upkeep (thousand) |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Shade sail with posts | 9.0 | At once | 0.6 |
| Three large-stock trees with tree pits | 6.0 | After 8-12 years | 0.3 |
| Grass in place of asphalt | 4.0 | After one season | 0.5 |

Source: city grounds contractors' price list, 2026 (sample)

Sails come down each autumn and last eight to ten years. Trees are cheapest and grow more effective each year, but shade little in their first summers. Grass needs watering in dry spells to keep its cooling effect.

::: callout kind=note title="Scope of the measurements"
The sensors measured surface temperature only. Air temperature at child height, radiant heat and ultraviolet exposure were not recorded.
:::

# Discussion

Shade over the surfaces children use is the measure that changed both temperature and behaviour, and mature trees did best across the whole afternoon. Two limits apply. The schools were not chosen at random, and those with mature trees tended to have larger grounds. And two summers is a short record.

# Recommendations

Order matters. The findings suggest a sequence for a school planning changes to its playground. First, shade the rubber surfaces under play equipment, with a sail if no tree can reach them, since these are the hottest patches and the ones children touch. Second, plant trees along the south and west edges of the main asphalt area now, accepting that their shade will come in later years. Third, replace little-used asphalt with grass only where it can be watered.

# Further work

The sensors stay for a third summer. A follow-up study will add air temperature at one metre and radiant heat measured with globe thermometers.
